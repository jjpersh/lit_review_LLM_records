## 2. Eligibility
Eligible, core. A generative LLM (LLaMa 3.2, 3B parameters, run locally through Ollama) is placed in a closed loop on a simulated nonlinear water tank. It is tested in two roles: as the sole controller writing the pump command, and as a supervisor that retunes a PID controller every 0.5 s (pp.2, 5–6). The plant is the MathWorks water-tank benchmark, a standard process benchmark (p.2). Not adjacent.

## 3. What was built, in plain words
The plant is a simulated single tank in MATLAB/Simulink 2025b. A pump fills it and water drains through an orifice at the bottom: A dH/dt = bV − a√H (Eq. 1, p.2). The level setpoint is 10 m. Each run lasts 60 s on a 0.1 s fixed-step solver, giving 600 samples (p.2). The pump command u is continuous, normalized to [0, 1] (pp.5, 8).

MATLAB calls Python 3.12.6 through its `py` interface. Python turns the numbers into a short text prompt and sends it to LLaMa 3.2 3B on a local workstation through Ollama v0.12.3 (Ollama is a local runtime that serves open-weight models through an API). A regular-expression parser pulls numbers from the reply and returns them to MATLAB (pp.4–5).

Four controllers share the same plant (pp.5–6):
1. **Conventional PID:** Ziegler–Nichols step-response gains, then hand-refined, fixed during the run.
2. **Autotuned PID:** gains from MATLAB PID Tuner, which linearizes the plant at the operating point; fixed during the run.
3. **Direct LLM:** no PID. At each simulation step the LLM gets the level and setpoint and returns a valve opening. This is clamped to [0, 1] and applied directly. The model has no memory of earlier steps. The authors call this a "negative baseline" (p.5).
4. **Hybrid LLM–PID:** a normal PID moves the pump. Every 0.5 s the LLM gets only the current error and returns four PID settings: Kp, Ki, Kd and the derivative-filter coefficient N. The new gains take effect one period later and are held between updates (p.6).

The check between the LLM and the plant is a parser. It accepts a gain set only if it is complete and finite. Otherwise the last valid set is kept (p.12). Nothing limits gain size or how fast gains change (p.10). No human approves the gains. The model is not fine-tuned. Prompt templates are shown as figures (Figs. 7–8). The decoding settings are said to be "fixed" but are not listed (p.4). Code is on GitHub (p.2).

## 4. Each tested setup
**Setup A: direct LLM actuation.** Nominal setpoint tracking only (start near empty, setpoint 10 m). The paper says "three runs for each experiment" (p.7), mean ± SD. Comparators are the ZN-refined fixed PID and the MATLAB-autotuned PID, both tuned non-LLM baselines. Metrics: rise time, peak time, overshoot, settling time, steady-state error (SSE), integrated absolute error (IAE) and total variation of the control signal (TVu, the sum of |u(k+1) − u(k)|; lower means smoother action) (p.7). The direct LLM is left out of the bias and drift tests (Tables 2–3, pp.10–11).

**Setup B: hybrid LLM–PID (supervisory gain tuning).** Three scenarios, each run three times (pp.6–8):
- nominal tracking
- a +2 m step bias on the level sensor at t = 10 s
- a 1 m/s ramp drift on the level sensor from t = 10 s

The comparators and metrics are the same as Setup A. Bias runs also report maximum deviation, recovery time and final level. Drift runs also report peak level, time to empty and maximum deviation. The same LLM (LLaMa 3.2 3B) is used throughout; no other model was tested.

## 5. Results
- **Direct LLM fails (nominal).** "The resulting SSE was approximately 8.5 m. The extremely large IAE (510.7)" (p.8). The PIDs score IAE 74.1 ± 0.4 and 60.3 ± 0.3 (Table 1, p.8). The LLM's commands sit near "u ≈ 0.12" (p.8). The level never nears the setpoint, so rise time and settling time are not reported.
- **Hybrid is fastest under nominal tracking.** "rapid rise time of approximately 3.5 s ... Overshoot was limited to about 2 %, and the settling time was reduced to 13.5 s" (p.8). For comparison:
  - conventional PID: rise 6.5 s, about 30% overshoot, settling 40.2 s
  - autotuned PID: rise 4.5 s, no overshoot, settling 25.1 s, SSE 0.2 m
  - hybrid: IAE 49.2 ± 3.1 against 74.1 and 60.3, but TVu 28.5 ± 4.6 against 16.4 and 11.8 (Table 1, p.8)

  So the LLM beats a tuned non-LLM baseline on IAE and speed, but its control action is rougher.
- **Sensor bias.** "The recovery time is 12.1 s, representing the fastest adjustment" (p.10). Autotuned PID takes 16.2 s and conventional PID 38.5 s. Hybrid IAE is 38.9 ± 2.4 against 41.5 ± 0.4 (autotuned) and 64.2 ± 0.6 (conventional). Hybrid TVu is 3.1 ± 0.5 against 2.3 ± 0.1 (autotuned). Max deviation is 2.6 m for the hybrid against 1.9 m for autotuned (Table 2, p.10, rebuilt from the text). All three controllers settle the true level at about 8 m because none detects the fault (p.10).
- **Sensor drift: the hybrid is worst.** All three controllers drain the tank. After that, the LLM keeps changing gains and causes "large spikes in the response, with peak excursions exceeding 20 m" (p.10). Hybrid IAE is 538.1 ± 14.6 against 452.6 ± 2.7 (autotuned) and 468.3 ± 3.2 (conventional). Hybrid TVu is 97.2 ± 18.5 against 11.8 ± 0.4 and 16.5 ± 0.5 (Table 3, p.11).
- **Gain behaviour.** Under nominal tracking, the error is within ±0.2 m after about 4 s. Even so, Kp spikes from about 5 to 20 and "increasing to about 40 by t ≈ 10 s" (p.12). The authors put the nominal gain down to "transient gain amplification rather than enhanced robustness" (p.13). There is no fixed-gain PID tuned more aggressively and no adaptive controller to separate that effect (pp.12, 14).
- **Timing.** "each LLM query–response cycle required approximately 0.3 s", below the 0.5 s supervisory period (p.6).
- No count or rate of malformed, rejected or harmful LLM outputs is reported.
- The paper is inconsistent about the start level: "initialized empty" (p.2) but "initialized at a low liquid level (1 m)" (p.8).

## 6. Duplicates
The experiment overlaps with S203 (Wen and Sajid 2026, "Emerging risks and failure boundaries of applying LLMs to process control", Digital Chemical Engineering). Both papers use:
- the same first author
- the same simulated water tank (setpoint 10 m, start near 1 m)
- LLaMa 3.2 3B through Ollama on a MATLAB/Simulink 2025b–Python bridge
- the same direct-actuation and 0.5 s supervisory PID-retuning setups

S203 adds a multivariable CSTR case. S033 is the fuller report of the tank experiment: three runs, quantitative tables and an autotuned PID baseline. Count the tank result once.

## 7. Terms to define
- Hybrid LLM–PID: a standard PID moves the pump, and the LLM rewrites its gains every 0.5 s.
- Direct LLM controller: the LLM writes the pump valve opening itself, with no PID in the loop.
- Derivative filter coefficient N: the bandwidth setting of the first-order filter on the D term; the LLM also sets it.
- TVu (total variation of u): the sum of absolute step-to-step changes in the pump command; higher means more actuator movement.
- IAE: integrated absolute tracking error over the 60 s run.
- Measurement bias / drift: a step (+2 m) or ramp (1 m/s) offset added to the level sensor reading, while the true level is unaffected.
- Ollama: a local runtime that serves open-weight LLMs through an API.
- Supervisory sampling interval: the 0.5 s period between LLM gain updates. It is slower than the 0.1 s solver step and set above the worst observed LLM latency.

## 9. Manuscript text

**4.3 Supervisory and advanced control (LLM-facilitated hybrid control)**

Short version: Wen et al. put a 3B-parameter LLaMa model above a PID loop on a simulated water tank. The model retuned the PID gains every 0.5 s. This cut rise time to about 3.5 s, against 4.5 s for a MATLAB-autotuned PID, and cut IAE to 49.2 against 60.3. <!-- p.8 -->

Long version: Wen et al. tested a 3B-parameter LLaMa model in a simulated nonlinear water tank. The model ran locally and retuned a PID controller every 0.5 s. Its comparators were a Ziegler–Nichols PID and a MATLAB-autotuned PID. <!-- p.5 --> In nominal tracking, the hybrid had the lowest IAE (49.2 ± 3.1 against 60.3 ± 0.3 for the autotuned PID, three runs each). It also used more than twice the actuator movement (TVu 28.5 against 11.8). <!-- p.8 --> Under a +2 m sensor bias it recovered fastest (12.1 s against 16.2 s). <!-- p.10 --> Under a ramp sensor drift it was the worst of the three: IAE 538.1 against 452.6, TVu 97.2 against 11.8. Its gains kept spiking after the tank was empty. <!-- p.11 --> The authors put the nominal gain down to "transient gain amplification", not robustness. <!-- p.13 -->

**4.4 Direct actuation**

Short version: On the same simulated tank, the same LLM wrote the pump valve opening directly at each step. It never reached the 10 m setpoint: steady-state error was about 8.5 m and IAE was 510.7, against 60.3 for an autotuned PID. <!-- p.8 -->

Long version: Wen et al. ran a direct-actuation arm as a deliberate negative baseline. At each simulation step, LLaMa 3.2 (3B) received the tank level and setpoint and returned a valve opening. The opening was clamped to [0, 1] and applied with no PID and no memory of earlier steps. <!-- p.5 --> The commands stayed near 0.12. The level stalled about 8.5 m below the setpoint, with IAE 510.7 ± 12.6 against 60.3 ± 0.3 for the autotuned PID over three runs. <!-- p.8 --> The direct arm was not tried under sensor faults. <!-- p.10 --> This is one of the few process-control papers that reports a direct-actuation LLM failing against a tuned PID on the same plant.

**5.1 Head-to-head evidence**

Short version: Wen et al. are among the few to compare LLM-supervised PID tuning with a properly autotuned PID on the same plant. The LLM version was faster in nominal tracking (IAE 49.2 against 60.3). It was worse under sensor drift (IAE 538.1 against 452.6). <!-- p.8 --> <!-- p.11 -->

Long version: The comparison is fair as far as it goes. All four controllers share the same simulated tank, scenarios and metrics, and each result is the mean of three runs. One baseline is a MATLAB PID Tuner design. <!-- p.5 --> The outcome depends on conditions:
- The LLM-tuned PID wins on IAE in nominal tracking (49.2 against 60.3) and under a step bias (38.9 against 41.5). <!-- p.8 --> <!-- p.10 -->
- It loses under ramp drift (538.1 against 452.6). <!-- p.11 -->
- It always moves the actuator more.

No adaptive controller and no more aggressive fixed-gain PID was tested. The authors concede that the nominal gain may come from higher gains alone, not from the LLM's reasoning. <!-- p.12 --> <!-- p.14 -->

**5.2 Safeguards**

Short version: Wen et al. checked LLM gain sets only for format and finiteness, and kept the last valid set otherwise. No bound limited gain size or rate of change. Under sensor drift, unbounded LLM gains caused level spikes above 20 m. <!-- p.10 --> <!-- p.12 -->

Long version: The study shows what happens when an LLM's numeric output passes through a syntax check only. The parser rejected malformed or non-finite gain sets and kept the previous valid set. <!-- p.12 --> It did nothing about values that were valid but aggressive. Under nominal tracking, Kp rose to about 40 while the error was within ±0.2 m. <!-- p.12 --> Under ramp sensor drift, the gains caused level spikes above 20 m and a TVu of 97.2 against 11.8 for the autotuned PID. <!-- p.10 --> <!-- p.11 --> The authors list gain-rate limits, parameter saturation and stability-aware filtering as needed future work. <!-- p.13 --> <!-- p.14 --> No safeguard was tested against bad proposals.

**5.3 Timing**

Short version: Each LLaMa 3.2 (3B) query took about 0.3 s. The authors therefore set the supervisory gain update to 0.5 s, with a 0.1 s simulation step and a one-period hold on new gains. <!-- p.6 -->

Long version: not needed.

**5.4 Local and open-weight models**

Short version: Wen et al. ran a 3B-parameter open-weight LLaMa 3.2 locally through Ollama, citing reproducibility and data privacy. The model was good enough to speed up nominal PID tracking, but it failed as a direct controller. <!-- p.4 --> <!-- p.8 -->
