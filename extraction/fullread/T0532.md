## 2. Eligibility
Yes. A generative LLM (LLaMA-8B) is connected to a control layer in two tested setups. It turns natural-language context into disturbance forecasts, and an MPC uses those forecasts online to compute its control input. Neither plant is a process system. One is a 2D robot tracking task (robotics, adjacent). The other is campus building energy management with a battery and PV (building energy, adjacent). Both are simulations. The paper also gives a theoretical regret bound for linear dynamics, but the experiments make it more than a position paper, so it is graded as core.

## 3. What was built, in plain words
InstructMPC wraps a conventional model predictive controller (MPC). The MPC alone computes the control input u_t that goes to the plant. The LLM never touches the actuator. The LLM sits in a "Language-to-Distribution" (L2D) module upstream of the MPC. At each step the L2D module receives text context c_t. Examples are "At t = 20, a gust of wind blows toward the lower left" or "The CDC conference deadline is approaching..." (p.6). The LLM is asked to fill a [MASK] in a prompt template: "[task description]. Given the current context: [context description], the expected scenario is [MASK]" (pp.4-5). Each candidate scenario token from a predefined set S is scored, and a softmax turns the scores into probabilities p(s|c_t). The predicted disturbance trajectory over the k-step horizon is the probability-weighted sum of historical disturbance trajectories, one per scenario (p.3). The MPC then solves its finite-horizon problem with that forecast as the disturbance term (p.2). There is no validator, gate or human approval on the forecast. Its only bound is that it is a convex combination of stored trajectories.

After k steps the true disturbance is revealed. Scenarios are then ranked by their distance to the realized disturbance, which yields preference pairs. LLaMA-8B is fine-tuned with Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) in batches once 240 pairs (robot) or 500 pairs (energy) have accumulated (p.5). Actuation is continuous: the robot's velocity input, and a battery charging rate in [0, 1]. A "zero-shot" arm uses the same pipeline without fine-tuning.

## 4. Each tested setup
**Setup A: robot tracking (simulated, p.5-6).** The plant is a linear 2D point robot, with p_{t+1} = p_t + 0.2 v_t. It tracks an unknown astroid path, and the target is revealed only just before each control decision. Wind disturbance is drawn from U(-2, 2) normally and from U(-45, 45) at 10 chosen time steps, with text describing the gust direction. The MPC uses quadratic cost with Q = diag(1, 1, 0, 0) and R = 10^-2 I. Three arms are compared:
- classic MPC with zero disturbance prediction (the paper calls this equivalent to LQR);
- MPC with predictions from zero-shot LLaMA-8B;
- InstructMPC with LLaMA-8B fine-tuned by DPO.

There were 20 episodes per method, shown as an average trajectory with a variance shadow (p.6). No numeric tracking metric is reported; the result is a plot only. The comparator is a nominal MPC with no disturbance forecast, not a non-LLM forecaster.

**Setup B: building energy management (simulated, p.6-7).** The plant is a battery SoC with unspecified nonlinear dynamics f(x, u) plus a disturbance from PV and demand. The workload is synthetic (1 task per unit time, demand drawn from U(1, 5) × 0.5 kWh). PV is modeled with PVLIB on 500 randomly sampled days of real weather. The cost is piecewise linear and reflects peak and valley electricity prices. The same three arms are compared. The metric is daily and total electricity cost over 500 days. No repeats or seeds are reported, and the battery model and price function are not given.

## 5. Results
- **Energy, total cost (Fig. 3 table, p.7, read from the image):** "Classic MPC 910.87 0.00%; Zero-Shot Prompting 512.54 43.73%; InstructMPC (fine-tuning) 379.96 58.29%". This is total electricity cost in dollars over 500 simulated days, and lower is better. The reduction is relative to classic MPC with zero forecast.
- **Energy, text claim (p.6):** the daily costs over 500 days demonstrate "the effectiveness of incorporating contextual predictions and model parameter adjustment". No variance or statistical test is given.
- **Robot (p.5):** "both methods with L2D predictions improve the performance of the robot, with the fine-tuned version demonstrating a closer robot path". This is qualitative and based on Fig. 2 (20 episodes per method); no error number is reported.
- **Theory (p.4):** with k = log_{1/ρ} T, the regret bound is O(√T log T) for linear dynamics. This is a bound, not a measurement.
- **Does the LLM beat a non-LLM alternative?** Only against MPC with no disturbance forecast. On cost, both LLM arms beat it, by 43.7% and 58.3%. The paper does not compare against a non-LLM forecaster, such as a time-series or historical-average predictor, or against an oracle or perfect-forecast MPC. So the size of the gain reflects mainly the value of having any forecast at all.
- **Authors' stated limitations (p.6):** the theory covers only linear dynamics. They plan "more experiments under different key parameters, such as the prediction horizon k and the choice of LLM". They also plan deployment on the OpenCEM physical testbed "to assess real-world generalization beyond synthetic workloads".

## 7. Terms to define
- MPC (model predictive control): repeatedly solves a finite-horizon optimal control problem and applies only the first input.
- L2D (Language-to-Distribution) module: an LLM that maps text context to probabilities over predefined disturbance scenarios, then to a weighted disturbance forecast.
- DPO (Direct Preference Optimization): fine-tunes an LLM from pairs of preferred and dispreferred outputs. Here the pairs are scenarios ranked by distance to the realized disturbance.
- PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization): a reinforcement-learning fine-tuning method. It is mentioned as an option but not used in the experiments.
- Zero-shot prompting: using the pretrained LLM with no fine-tuning.
- Regret: the cumulative cost of the learned forecaster's parameters minus the cost of the best fixed parameters in hindsight.
- Loss discrepancy: the gap between the gradient of the surrogate fine-tuning loss and that of the true MPC cost.
- DARE (discrete algebraic Riccati equation): gives the terminal cost matrix P.
- PVLIB: an open-source Python simulator for photovoltaic power output.
- SoC (state of charge): the battery charge fraction in [0, 1].
