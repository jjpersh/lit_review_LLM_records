# S203: Wen and Sajid 2026, risks and failure boundaries of LLMs in process control

## 1. Identity
- First author: He Wen (with Zaman Sajid, corresponding author)
- Year: 2026
- Title as printed: "Emerging risks and failure boundaries of applying large language models (LLMs) to process control"
- Venue: Digital Chemical Engineering 20 (2026) 100334 (Elsevier/IChemE), article type "Perspectives"; doi 10.1016/j.dche.2026.100334
- Peer-reviewed journal article (received 16 July 2026, accepted 12 August 2026). It is labelled a perspective, but it reports its own experiments.
- Page count: 12
- All 12 pages were read: the full clean text, plus page images of p.3 (Fig. 3) and p.4 (Fig. 4) to check the curves.

## 2. Eligibility
Yes, it is eligible. The paper tests a generative LLM (Llama 3.2 3B run through Ollama) in four staged setups:
- the LLM writes the pump command directly as free text
- the LLM writes the pump command as a constrained number
- the LLM retunes a PID controller every 0.5 s on a simulated water tank
- the same supervisory tuning on a simulated multivariable CSTR

These are process systems. The article type is "Perspectives" and the experiments are framed as "a preliminary reflection" (p.2). Even so, it has tested setups with closed-loop simulation results, so I recommend `core`. The evidence is thin: results are mostly qualitative and shown as single trajectories.

## 3. What was built, in plain words
The plant is a simulated single-tank water-level system in MATLAB/Simulink 2025b. The level setpoint is 10 m, starting from about 1 m. The pump command u is a normalized value between 0 and 1, and actuation is continuous. The second plant is a simulated multivariable CSTR. The paper does not give its model, inputs or outputs beyond temperature and concentration setpoints.

Process data move along a MATLAB→Python→Ollama bridge (Fig. 2, p.3):
1. Python 3.12.6 turns the process variables into a text prompt.
2. The prompt goes to Llama 3.2 3B running locally under Ollama (temperature 0.7, top-p 0.9).
3. Python parses the reply back into numbers and returns them to Simulink.

How the four trials use the LLM:
- **Trials 1 and 2:** the LLM is the controller. It receives the current and target level. In Trial 1 it may answer freely. In Trial 2 the prompt says "Output only a single actuator command between 0 and 1", and the parsed value is "directly transmitted to the control loop" (p.11). No validator, clipping or gate is described beyond the parser.
- **Trial 3:** a conventional PID controller moves the pump. Every 0.5 s the LLM receives level, target and error and returns new Kp, Ki and Kd, which are applied directly to the PID with no bounds or approval (p.3, p.12).
- **Trial 4:** the LLM receives reactor temperature, concentration and their setpoints and returns PID parameters for each loop of a multi-loop PID (p.12).

The model has no fine-tuning, no tools and no memory. The prompts are zero-shot and were not systematically tuned (p.12). Eight other open-source LLMs were tried in "preliminary exploratory tests" that are not reported quantitatively (p.2, p.8).

## 4. Each tested setup
1. **Trial 1, direct free-text control (water tank, simulated).**
   - Nominal setpoint step from about 1 m to 10 m over 60 s; one trajectory shown (Fig. 3, green).
   - Comparator: "conventional PID" (blue). The paper gives no tuning method, and the PID overshoots to about 12.5 m (Fig. 3).
   - No numerical metrics.
2. **Trial 2, numeric-constrained direct control.**
   - Same conditions, one trajectory (Fig. 3, black); same PID comparator; no metrics.
3. **Trial 3, LLM retuning of a SISO PID.**
   - Same conditions, one trajectory (Fig. 3, red) and gain trajectories (Fig. 4).
   - Supervisory update every 0.5 s. Inference latency was measured over 600 controller updates (Table 3, p.7).
   - Same PID comparator.
   - Nominal conditions only (p.4). No disturbance, fault or mismatch.
4. **Trial 4, LLM retuning of a multi-loop PID on a CSTR.**
   - Described only in text ("conceptually extended", p.3, p.7). No figure, data, number of runs or comparator is reported.

What was varied across trials is the LLM's authority and output format. No setup reports repeated runs or variance.

## 5. Results
- **Trial 1, failed.** "This configuration failed immediately at the implementation stage" (p.4). The level stays near its starting value of about 1 m (Fig. 3). This is qualitative: no count of unusable responses is given.
- **Trial 2, failed.** "The water level shows a slight upward trend ... but it still cannot reach the goal" (p.4). In Fig. 3 the level reaches about 1.6 m by 60 s, against a 10 m target. No numerical metric is given.
- **Trial 3, conditional success.**
  - The hybrid controller "achieved stable setpoint tracking under nominal conditions" (p.4).
  - Visually, in Fig. 3, it reaches 10 m in about 5 s with small ripples. The conventional PID overshoots to about 12.5 m and settles in about 30 s. The paper gives no numerical overshoot, IAE or settling time and does not claim superiority in words. A review can only say the figure *shows* faster, lower-overshoot tracking than an untuned-looking PID, in a single run.
  - Gains were "often irregular, abrupt", with spikes in Fig. 4: Kp up to about 40, Ki to about 15, Kd to about 20.
  - The authors themselves call it "conditional feasibility" (p.4).
- **Trial 3, latency.** "Across 600 controller updates, the mean latency was 0.30 s (median 0.15 s), with a range of 0.13 s to 3.01 s" (p.7).
  - Table 3 distribution: 81.5% under 0.2 s; 4.2% 0.2–0.5 s; 6.8% 0.5–1.0 s; 6.2% 1.0–2.0 s; 1.3% over 2.0 s.
  - The denominator is 600 updates. Lower is better.
  - So about 14% of updates exceeded the 0.5 s update interval.
- **Trial 4, failed.** "LLM-generated tuning recommendations became inconsistent and unstable" (p.4). This is qualitative only, with no data shown.
- **Other models.** Exploratory tests on Mistral 7B, Mixtral 8×7B, Qwen2.5-7B/14B, DeepSeek-V3, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B, Gemma 2 9B and Phi-4 "demonstrated similar limitations" (p.2). No numbers are given.
- **LLM versus non-LLM.** The paper does not quantitatively show the LLM beating a non-LLM alternative. Only Fig. 3, a single run with a PID of unreported tuning, visually favours Trial 3.
- **Main conclusions.** The paper's contribution is a taxonomy of five risks (Table 2, p.5): symbolic-to-physical, physics grounding, control stability, temporal compatibility and multivariable reasoning. It splits them into LLM-native and LLM-amplified (Table 5, p.9) and concludes that LLMs suit "supervisory and decision-support roles" (p.1).
- **Limitations the authors state:**
  - The experiments are "not ... an exhaustive evaluation of all possible LLM-control architectures" (p.5).
  - The study "does not constitute a controlled model-scaling investigation" (p.8).
  - "no systematic prompt-tuning procedure was performed" (p.12).

The water-tank LLM-PID experiment probably overlaps with those papers (same authors and the same MATLAB–Python–Ollama rig). The matching rec_ids are not known from this paper.

## 7. Terms to define
- Direct LLM control: the LLM output is used as the pump command itself.
- Numeric-constrained LLM control: the prompt forces a single number between 0 and 1, which is parsed and sent to the actuator.
- Hybrid LLM-PID supervisory control: a PID moves the actuator while the LLM rewrites Kp, Ki and Kd every 0.5 s.
- Symbolic-to-physical risk: the gap between text tokens and machine-executable control signals.
- Physics grounding risk: numeric outputs are not based on plant dynamics.
- Control stability risk: LLM gain updates follow no bounded adaptation law.
- Temporal compatibility risk: inference latency and its jitter, compared with process time scales.
- Multivariable reasoning risk: the LLM cannot coordinate coupled loops.
- LLM-native vs LLM-amplified risk: a risk that exists only because of the LLM, versus a general data-driven-control risk that the LLM mechanism makes worse.
- τLLM: the total delay from serialization, communication, inference and parsing.
